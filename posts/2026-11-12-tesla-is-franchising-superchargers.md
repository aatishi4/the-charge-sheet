In the third quarter, Tesla added a net **2,108 Supercharger stalls**. That's **41% fewer** than a year earlier and the slowest third quarter since 2021, per [State of Charge's read of Tesla's Q3 numbers](https://evchargingstations.com/chargingnews/tesla-supercharger-q3-2026/). In the same quarter, the network logged a record **69 million sessions**, up **29%** ([State of Charge](https://evchargingstations.com/chargingnews/tesla-supercharging-q3-2026/)).

Paren's count points the same way. Tesla built only **34%** of the new US fast-charging ports in Q3, and its share of all US ports slipped below half, to **49.3%** from 54.3% a year ago ([Paren via EV Infrastructure News](https://www.evinfrastructurenews.com/charging-installation/us-fast-charging-network-q3-2026-tesla-market-share)).

Less building, more use out of what's already there, and a program where somebody else pays for the new stalls. That isn't Tesla losing interest in charging. That's Tesla going McDonald's.

## Tesla doesn't want to sell electricity

Tesla doesn't want to sell electricity to cars any more than McDonald's wants to flip hamburgers. It still wants to be part of the conversation, and it built something that's really good: the Supercharger network. Early on, Tesla owned the sites and partnered with people to get them built. Now it's letting qualified people take the program and run with it.

That program is [Supercharger for Business](https://www.tesla.com/learn/supercharger-for-business), launched in [September 2025](https://driveteslacanada.ca/news/tesla-opens-supercharger-for-business-program-letting-companies-buy-and-host-their-own-chargers/). The host buys at least four posts, pays for installation, sets the price and keeps the site clean. Tesla designs the hardware, monitors the site around the clock, services it, answers the driver hotline and puts it in the navigation. For revenue sites, Tesla takes an all-inclusive **$0.10/kWh**, according to [Electrek's look at the configurator](https://electrek.co/2026/04/08/tesla-supercharger-for-business-configurator-pricing/), which put a standard eight-post V4 site at about **$940,000** installed. That dime is the royalty.

When I was running chargers, we never really wanted to sell chargers. It was always own and operate, to a degree. But at some point you're not just owning chargers and operating them. You're owning the brand, and the person on site is the franchisee.

## The McDonald's play

About [**95%** of McDonald's restaurants](https://corporate.mcdonalds.com/corpmcd/franchising-overview.html) are owned and operated by independent local business owners. McDonald's still owns the IP and the product, and in reality it's still running the system. Others do the grunt work. Early on McDonald's ran the restaurants itself. Over time it figured out there are plenty of people willing and able to run them, so why not let them and take a share off the top? Margin is margin.

Tesla is doing the same thing, and I think it's the smart play. It's the natural next step in scaling. You want to keep a relatively narrow lane in operations so things don't go bad when you spread too thin. Letting others play a part, while you keep a well-producing product, fortifies the business. Tesla keeps building the brand and stays in the business, at a high level: owning assets and contracts that produce cash flow without as much input, instead of carrying the full capex and opex rigor of owning and operating everything end to end.

## Good for hosts. Not for every host.

Is this bad for hosts who want to own and operate? No. A Tesla-branded station is a solid way to get charging up and running, with a brand drivers already trust.

But not everyone can run a McDonald's, or a Taco Bell, or a hotel, or a gas station. Producing cash flow doesn't make it a sure winner out of the gate. There's still a lot of operating to do. You have to be in the trenches, pushing things forward. That was fine for Tesla early on. At some point it becomes a lower priority for them, which is the whole point of handing it to you. And a new site [doesn't get ten sessions on day one](/blog/utilization-ramp/).

## What's missing: the franchise rules

Every franchise worth buying into has a bar to clear. McDonald's generally wants [**$500,000**](https://www.mcdonalds.com/us/en-us/faq/can-you-still-get-a-mcdonald-s-franchise--in-the-united-states--and-how-much-does-it-cost.html) of non-borrowed personal money just to consider you, and its [corporate franchising page](https://corporate.mcdonalds.com/corpmcd/franchising-overview.html) now says **$750,000**, plus significant business experience. Then comes [**6 to 12 months**](https://mcdonalds.com/content/dam/sites/usa/nfl/us-franchising/McDonalds-Franchisee-Overview-One-Sheeter.pdf) of training. Gas works the same way: [about 55%](https://www.convenience.org/topics/fuels-and-energy/who-sells-americas-fuel) of US stores that sell fuel are single-store operators, many under a big brand's canopy. Hotels, Valero, Shell: same idea.

I don't see that with Tesla. Its [program page](https://www.tesla.com/learn/supercharger-for-business) lists no liquidity, experience or site requirements. I don't see it with any charging brand, really. And at some point it's going to have to happen, because this is no different from a gas station brand, a hotel brand or a Taco Bell. It's owning and operating an asset that carries somebody else's brand.

What I'd want in a charging franchise agreement:

- Experience owning and operating sites. This is the key one.
- Enough liquidity to sustain the burn for two, three, four years, whatever it takes to ramp.
- Strict brand standards: batteries where they make sense, cleaning service, proper striping, proper lighting.
- Placement rules, so nobody stacks stalls in a bad spot or one on top of another.

This isn't about thinning the herd or keeping people out of the market. It's about tightening who gets access, so the people who get in can keep the brand alive and serve the customers who keep it alive. Requirements like these keep the hosts and the brand afloat by protecting the impression of the product and keeping it consistent from site to site. That's where charging needs to head if we want it to keep growing in a good way.

## What I'd do

If you're looking at Tesla-branded stalls, or any branded program, hold yourself to the franchise bar even though nobody is asking you to clear it yet:

- Have cash set aside for two to four years of ramp, on top of the build.
- Be honest about whether you've run a site before, or who on your team has.
- Budget for the lighting, striping and cleaning a franchisor would demand.
- Get the money lined up the way a franchisee would. Lenders [already treat you like one](/blog/nobody-lends-on-a-charger/).
